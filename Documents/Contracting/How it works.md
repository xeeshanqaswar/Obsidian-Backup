### How contracting actually works

The mental shift first, because everything else follows from it: **as an employee you sell attendance; as a contractor you sell a defined outcome.** Your employer bought your availability and absorbed the risk of things taking longer than expected. A client buys a specific deliverable, and the risk of it taking longer is now yours. That single change explains why contracts, scope documents, and deposits exist — they're all mechanisms for allocating that risk between you and the client.

**The two engagement shapes:**

_Time & materials (hourly)._ You track hours, invoice biweekly or monthly, get paid for what you worked. Low risk, low ceiling. Good for open-ended work where scope genuinely can't be pinned down, and good for you right now because it requires almost no commercial skill.

_Fixed price._ You quote a number for a defined deliverable. If you finish early you keep the margin; if you underestimate, you eat it. This is where the money is, but only once you can estimate accurately — which takes maybe five projects.

**The paper trail, in the order it happens:**

1. **Discovery call.** 20–30 minutes. You're diagnosing, not pitching. What are they building, what's the deadline, what's failed before, who decides, what's the budget range. Ask about budget early and directly — "what range were you thinking for this?" — because it saves you both time.
2. **Proposal / Statement of Work.** One to two pages. Deliverables listed explicitly, timeline, price, payment schedule, number of revision rounds included, and — most important — an **out of scope** section listing what you are _not_ doing. That section is what you point at later when someone asks for "just one small addition."
3. **Contract.** For anything above a couple thousand dollars, get a real one. It needs: payment terms, **IP transfers to client only upon receipt of final payment**, confidentiality, a liability cap (cap it at fees paid), termination clause with a kill fee, and governing law. Don't draft this yourself. Use a template — Bonsai and Docracy both have decent freelance/dev agreements, and "The Contract Killer" is a well-known plain-language template. Many studios will send you _their_ MSA instead, which is usually fine; the clauses to actually read are IP ownership scope, non-compete, and payment terms.
4. **Deposit.** 50% upfront for a new fixed-price client, or milestones at 40/30/30. This is standard and nobody blinks. If a client refuses any upfront payment, that is information about how the rest of the engagement will go.
5. **Deliver.** For fixed-price, deliver a _build_ for review. Source code and project files go over after final payment clears. This isn't adversarial, it's normal, and stating it upfront in the SOW means it never becomes a conversation.
6. **Invoice.** Numbered sequentially, your name/address/NTN, client details, line items, currency (USD), payment terms (Net 15 for small clients, Net 30 if they insist), and bank/Wise details. Keep every invoice — FBR will want them.

**The thing that actually kills contractor margins** is not bad rates, it's scope creep. Every fixed-price project will have a moment where the client asks for something reasonable-sounding that wasn't in the SOW. The correct response is warm and immediate: _"Happy to do that — it's outside what we scoped, so let me send a small change order for it."_ Say it the first time and it never becomes a pattern. Say yes the first time and you've taught them the scope is negotiable.

**Money rails:** Wise Business or Payoneer for receiving, quote everything in USD, and route foreign income through formal banking channels — which brings us to PSEB.
