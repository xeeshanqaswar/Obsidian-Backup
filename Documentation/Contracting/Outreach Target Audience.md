### Who to target, in priority order:

_White-label playable studios_ are your fastest path, because they have existing demand and chronic capacity crunches. eJaw explicitly positions itself as a white-label production partner for agencies, working under NDA with either direct client relationships or fully invisible collaboration. Playable Factory (Istanbul, works with Zynga), AdLiven, and Studio Cation are in the same category — Studio Cation actively recruits playable ads developers for a continuous stream of client work. Approaching these is low-resistance: you're not selling, you're offering overflow capacity. [EJAW](https://ejaw.net/playable-ads-development/)[Studiocation](https://studiocation.com/jobs/playable_ads_developer/)

_UA managers and performance marketing leads_ at mid-size mobile studios. Higher rates, longer sales cycle. This is the direct-client path.

_Game dev outsourcing studios_ in Eastern Europe, Turkey, and the UK who need Unity overflow. They subcontract constantly.

_Curated hourly platforms_ — Arc, Toptal, Braintrust — for your cash floor. Arc even has a playable-ads hiring category, which tells you the demand exists there.

**How to find them:** Clutch.co is the best single source — filter for game development and advertising agencies, sort by size, and you get company names with employee counts and contact paths. Beyond that: LinkedIn search for "UA Manager" or "Creative Producer" at mobile studios; the credits of games in genres you know; job boards like gamejobs.co, where a studio posting for a playable ads developer is a studio with more work than people.

**The message.** Short, specific, one link, tiny ask. To a studio:

> Hi [Name] — saw [Studio] is running UA for [Game]. I build HTML5 playables: [link]. Sub-5MB with real physics, wrapped for AppLovin, Mintegral, and IronSource. Background is 8 years Unity — shipped Prison Escape (5M downloads) and GI Joe for Hasbro. If you're testing new creative concepts this quarter, one-week turnaround. Want me to send three examples?

To a white-label studio, the angle inverts:

> Hi [Name] — I'm a senior Unity engineer in Lahore (UTC+5) with playable experience. I know your pipeline runs hot in Q3/Q4. If you ever need overflow capacity on playables or prototypes, I work white-label under NDA and can turn a single-mechanic playable in a week. Portfolio: [link]. Happy to do a paid test build.

Note what neither message does: ask for a call. Cold outreach converts when the ask is smaller than the cost of saying yes.

### How to learn this properly

The volume approach beats the study approach — send 10–15 a week, log them in a spreadsheet, change one variable at a time, and you'll learn more in a month than from any book. But two are worth reading: Blair Enns' _The Win Without Pitching Manifesto_ (short, and it will change how you handle clients who want free work), and Jonathan Stark's daily newsletter on value-based pricing, which is free and specifically about escaping the hourly trap you're going to hit.

Expect roughly 1 in 20 cold messages to convert to a conversation. Forty messages is two conversations, one of which becomes a client. That ratio is normal — the failure mode is sending eight, getting nothing, and concluding it doesn't work.

### Running it

You should be talking maybe 20% of the time. The instinct as a first-time contractor is to prove yourself — walk through your credentials, explain your approach. Resist it. They already looked at your portfolio; that's _why_ they took the call. What earns the deal is demonstrating that you understood the problem better than the other three people they spoke to.

Open by framing it so they know the shape: _**"I've got about twenty minutes. I mostly want to understand what you're building and what the constraints are, and then I'll tell you honestly whether I'm the right fit. Sound good?"**_ That last clause does a lot of work — it signals you might say no, which immediately makes you a consultant rather than a vendor.

Two moves that matter more than the questions themselves:

**Ask "what have you tried already?"** Nobody asks this. The answer tells you why the project exists, what the client is afraid of, and often what they'll pay to avoid repeating.

**Summarise back before you close.** _"Let me make sure I've got this — you need a swipe-mechanic playable for [game], under 5MB, running on AppLovin and Mintegral, live before the September test window, and the last vendor delivered something too heavy to pass QA. Is that right?"_ Most vendors never do this. It's the single highest-leverage thirty seconds of the call.

### On the budget question

Ask it plainly and without apology: _"What range were you thinking for this?"_ If they deflect, don't push — bracket it instead. _"For context, a single-mechanic playable with three variations usually lands between $2,500 and $4,000 depending on the art situation. Does that sit in the right zone?"_ Their reaction tells you what you need. Silence or a wince means you're above their range; instant agreement means you're below it.

### Closing the call — yes, exactly as you described

Don't quote a price on the call, even if you already know the number. Under live pressure you will anchor low, and you haven't yet thought about the risk factors. Taking it away also makes you look deliberate rather than eager.

The close is three parts, about ninety seconds:
- **Recap** what you heard, in one or two sentences.
- **Say whether you're a fit.** If it's a bad fit, say so on the call — it costs you nothing and it's remembered. Referring them elsewhere is how you get referrals back.
- **Commit to a specific deliverable on a specific date.** Not "I'll get back to you." Instead: _"I'll send you a written scope with fixed price, timeline, and payment terms by Thursday evening your time."_ Then one last question: _"Anything else that might change the shape of this that we haven't covered?"_ — which catches the surprise requirement that would otherwise wreck your estimate.

Then send it within 48 hours. Speed is a genuine competitive advantage here; agencies routinely take a week, and being the first credible proposal on someone's desk matters more than being the most polished.

`The section that will feel most awkward the first few times is the money question, and the section that will win you the most work is "what have you tried already." Push through the discomfort on the first and lean hard on the second.`
