### The review that actually matters

Setting cosmetics aside — the substantive problem with the site isn't errors, it's that **the whole page is built to get you hired, not to get you contracted.** That's structural, and no amount of cleanup fixes it.

Look at the sequence a visitor moves through: headline says "Senior Unity Engineer," primary button says "Download CV," then About, then Experience as a job history, then Education, then a Skills list. That's a résumé rendered as a website. It answers "should we interview this person for a full-time role?" A client has a different question — _can this person solve my specific problem, by when, for how much_ — and the page never addresses it.

**Your About copy is your weakest asset and your strongest opportunity.** Right now it says: scalable production-ready systems, multiplayer architecture, mobile optimization, clean maintainable code, systems thinker, values structure and clarity and collaboration. Every competent Unity dev on Earth writes that paragraph. Meanwhile, sitting unremarked in your Works section is a range almost nobody has: a Hasbro-licensed console beat 'em up with Photon Fusion, a 5M-download mobile title, shipped VR on both Meta and Steam, real-money multiplayer with the compliance weight that implies, and a cognitive-assessment simulation for the Singapore-ETH Centre. Research instrument to licensed console to real-money mobile is a genuinely unusual span. That's the paragraph. Lead with the proof, not the adjectives.

**Your Works entries describe the games rather than your contribution.** Prison Escape's copy is store marketing — stealth, wits, cunning. A client doesn't care. The GI Joe entry is the exception and it's your best one, precisely because it names what you owned and who you coordinated with. Rewrite the other six in that shape: what you were responsible for, what the hard constraint was, what shipped.

**Nothing tells a buyer how to buy.** No services, no engagement models, no price anchors, no availability, no timezone, no response time. A client evaluating three contractors picks the one who made the decision easy.

**Nothing is tappable.** For the playable-ad buyers we discussed, this is the whole game. They will not watch a video.

**The single-scroll structure works against you in sales.** Everything lives at one URL with anchor links, so you can't send a prospect straight to the thing that's relevant to them. Deep-linkable pages — `/playables`, `/services`, one URL per case study — turn the site from a brochure into something you can aim.

Minor, but while you're in there: "Cursor" sitting in a skills list beside Blender and Photoshop reads oddly, and "Client Handling, Team Leadership" is buried as the last bullet when for contract work it's a selling point that belongs in prose near the top.


### For Hiring Purposes 

**The contact section contrast.** A recruiter who wants to reach you cannot read your email address. This is still the worst thing on the page.

**The hover-only works grid.** In a hiring context this is more damaging than in a contracting one. A recruiter screening candidates spends 20–40 seconds per profile, frequently on a phone, in a batch of fifty. Your Hasbro-licensed console credit, your 5M-download title, and your Singapore-ETH research simulation are all invisible unless someone hovers with a mouse. Persistent captions under each tile — title, platform, your role, the number — is the highest-return change on the entire page.

### What's missing, specifically for Europe

Three real gaps for this purpose that I didn't flag before because I was reading it as a sales page.

**Your Experience section has no dates.** This is the biggest omission on the page. "Mindstorm Studios — Senior Software Engineer" with no years means a hiring manager cannot assess your tenure, your trajectory, or whether you job-hop. Recruiters read missing dates as concealment. Add years and company locations.

**No shipped-titles block.** In games hiring, shipped titles are the single most-scanned credential — more than skills, more than education. A compact list near the top (title, platform, your role, year) does more than three paragraphs of About copy.

### The part that actually determines the outcome

Your page is not your bottleneck. Sponsorship is. So it's worth knowing where you stand.

Germany is your strongest target. The 2026 standard Blue Card threshold is €50,700, with a reduced threshold of €45,934 for shortage occupations including IT. With a BSCS and eight years of senior experience you qualify comfortably, and €45.9K is very achievable for a senior Unity engineer in Germany. Practical note: processing times at German missions vary widely, typically four to twenty weeks depending on embassy workload — build that into your timeline. [Aldaglegal](https://aldaglegal.com/en/eu-blue-card-germany-2026/)[deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/nl/en/services/tax/perspectives/highly-skilled-migrant-and-european-blue-card-2026-salary-thresholds.html)

The Netherlands is a much higher bar. The Highly Skilled Migrant threshold is €5,942 gross per month for applicants 30 and over, excluding the 8% holiday allowance, effective 1 January 2026 — roughly €71K annually. Also, Dutch employers must be IND-recognised sponsors, which narrows the field considerably. France sits at €59,373. Poland and the Czech Republic have far lower thresholds and very large game industries, and are worth serious consideration even though the salaries are lower. [EP Advisory](https://ep-advisory.com/blog/work-visa-salary-thresholds-europe/)[Aldaglegal](https://aldaglegal.com/en/eu-blue-card-germany-2026/)

**One strategic thing worth weighing.** European games hiring has been contracting, and studios sitting on a pool of locally available laid-off talent are less inclined to take on sponsorship paperwork. That doesn't make it impossible, but it lengthens the odds.

Where your profile is unusually strong is the _non-games_ Unity market, and Germany is the world centre of it — automotive and industrial visualization, simulation, medical, training. Those employers sponsor more readily, pay well above the Blue Card threshold, and compete for far fewer candidates. And your S.P.A.C.E project, a cognitive-assessment simulation built for a research centre, is precisely the credential that market wants. Right now it's the eighth tile in an unlabeled grid. For a Europe job hunt I'd move it to the front and give it a real case study.
